CHAPTER 1
PROJECT AND ITS BACKGROUND
================================================================================

Project Context

Electricity is essential for daily life in Valencia City, Bukidnon. It powers
households, runs local businesses, supports schools, and keeps healthcare
facilities functioning. When unexpected power interruptions happen, daily
routines stop, businesses lose income, and community safety can be compromised.
During these brownouts and line faults, what residents need most is clear and
reliable information: which areas are affected, what caused the outage, and how
soon the power is expected to return.

In Valencia City, power distribution is managed by the First Bukidnon Electric
Cooperative (FIBECO). At present, when an outage strikes, residents usually turn
to informal and uncoordinated channels to report the problem or seek updates.
Many try calling the emergency hotline numbers, while others post complaints or
comment on social media pages such as Facebook. This setup creates significant
bottlenecks. During large outages, telephone lines quickly become congested with
busy signals, leaving many callers frustrated. On social media, hundreds of
comments pour in with incomplete information—often just stating "Walay suga diri"
without giving a specific purok, landmark, or photo of the problem.

For dispatchers and utility personnel, sorting through these scattered messages
is difficult and time-consuming. They must answer repeated calls about the same
tripped feeder while trying to figure out which specific neighborhood actually has
a localized problem, such as a fallen branch on a service line or a blown
transformer fuse. Because there is no single shared platform that maps these
reports geographically, dispatchers cannot easily tell whether thirty calls come
from thirty separate problems or from one single line fault affecting an entire
purok. As a result, field linemen often spend extra time searching for faults
on the ground, while residents remain in the dark about whether anyone is working
on their neighborhood.

To solve this problem, the researchers designed and developed "Valencia
PowerWatch: A Multi-Role Web- and Mobile-Based Community Power Interruption
Reporting, Verification, and Information Management System." The project gives
Valencia City an organized, map-based digital platform where residents, utility
dispatchers, and field crews can coordinate effectively. Instead of vague phone
calls, residents can submit clear reports with their exact barangay, purok, GPS
location, and photos. Dispatchers can see these reports grouped on a live city
map, verify them, and send field crews directly to the site. Meanwhile, linemen
can update repair progress from their mobile phones, allowing the system to
send automatic status updates back to residents through text messages and web
notifications.


Problem Statement

Currently, power interruption reporting and information tracking in Valencia City
relies on congested telephone hotlines and unmoderated social media threads. This
lack of an organized, geo-referenced system causes slow incident verification,
delayed crew dispatch, and public confusion during outages.

Specifically, this study aims to answer the following research questions:

1. What functional and operational requirements are needed to build a reliable
   system for submitting, verifying, assigning, and resolving power outage
   reports across the 31 barangays of Valencia City?
2. How can an interactive map and a deterministic Estimated Time of Restoration
   (ETR) algorithm be developed to visualize outage clusters and provide realistic
   restoration timelines for residents and utility staff?
3. How can a multi-portal architecture with role-based access control and
   automated notifications (in-app alerts, SMS, and a bilingual chatbot) be
   implemented to support residents, dispatchers, and field linemen?
4. How acceptable and effective is the developed Valencia PowerWatch system
   when evaluated by community residents, utility staff, and IT experts using
   the ISO/IEC 25010 Software Quality Standard?


Objectives of the Study

General Objective

To design, develop, and evaluate "Valencia PowerWatch," an integrated multi-role
web- and mobile-based community power interruption reporting, verification, and
information management system for Valencia City, Bukidnon.

Specific Objectives

1. To identify and analyze the user requirements, reporting workflows, and
   operational needs of Valencia City residents, utility dispatchers, and field
   crews through interviews and process walkthroughs.
2. To design and implement a responsive geospatial mapping module using
   Leaflet.js and a deterministic Estimated Time of Restoration (ETR) engine
   based on reported fault types, weather conditions, and incident density.
3. To develop the three dedicated portals (Resident Community Portal, Dispatcher
   Admin Dashboard, and Field Staff Mobile App) with role-based security,
   automated SMS text alerts via Semaphore API, and a bilingual Cebuano/English
   assistance chatbot.
4. To evaluate the performance, usability, and technical quality of the developed
   system using an evaluation instrument based on the ISO/IEC 25010 Software
   Quality Standard administered to residents, utility personnel, and IT experts.


Significance of the Study

This project offers practical benefits to several groups within the community:

Community Residents of Valencia City. Residents gain a convenient, 24/7 web-based
tool to report power outages from their smartphones or computers. Instead of
wondering whether the utility knows about their situation, residents can see
their report status change in real time (from Submitted to Verified, In Progress,
and Resolved), receive SMS updates when technicians arrive, and view scheduled
maintenance advisories before work begins.

Utility Dispatchers and Office Staff. Dispatchers receive an organized dashboard
that groups multiple calls from the same area under a single incident ticket.
This eliminates duplicate record-keeping, helps staff assess the number of
affected households faster, and allows them to assign field crews based on which
barangays need the most urgent attention.

Field Maintenance Crews and Linemen. Linemen can use the mobile-optimized Staff
Portal to check their assigned jobs, view attached photos and exact purok
landmarks, confirm their arrival on site using GPS, and upload completion photos
once repairs are finished. This reduces confusion and minimizes travel delays.

Local Government Units and Disaster Responders (CDRRMO). City officials and
disaster teams can look at the city-wide outage map during storms to identify
which barangays have lost power. This helps them prioritize emergency resources,
such as deploying backup generators to health centers, water pumping stations,
and evacuation facilities.

Future IT Researchers. This project provides a practical reference for other
students building civic software, showing how to implement Progressive Web
Applications (PWAs), geocoding with OpenStreetMap, and hybrid database models
that combine fast local storage with cloud-backed backups.


Scope and limitations

Scope

The project covers the development, deployment, and evaluation of the Valencia
PowerWatch system for all thirty-one (31) official barangays of Valencia City,
Bukidnon: Bagontaas, Banlag, Barobo, Batangan, Catumbalon, Colonia, Concepcion,
Dagat-Kidavao, Guinoyuran, Kahapunan, Laligan, Lilingayon, Lourdes, Lumbayao,
Lumbo, Lurogan, Maapag, Mabuhay, Mailag, Mt. Nebo, Nabago, Pinatilan, Poblacion,
San Carlos, San Isidro, Sinabuagan, Sinayawan, Sugod, Tongantongan, Tugaya, and
Vintar.

The system includes the following components:
1. User Roles: Four roles are supported—Resident, Field Personnel (Staff),
   Utility / Dispatcher, and System Administrator.
2. Core Features:
   - Outage reporting with GPS location, purok selection, fault description,
     and photo or video attachments.
   - Centralized case management where dispatchers group related reports under
     master incident codes (`OUT-XXXX`).
   - Interactive GIS map showing active outages, restoration heatmaps, and
     barangay boundaries using Leaflet.js.
   - Algorithmic Estimated Time of Restoration (ETR) calculation using fault
     severity, report density, and live Open-Meteo weather codes.
   - Automated text alerts via the Semaphore SMS API and an in-app inbox.
   - A built-in rule-based chatbot answering questions in both English and
     Cebuano (Bisaya).
3. Technical Architecture: Built using Node.js and Express.js, running on
   Render.com, using a hybrid data setup with local SQLite (Write-Ahead Logging)
   for speed and Neon Serverless PostgreSQL for persistent cloud snapshots.
4. Quality Evaluation: Evaluated by fifty (50) selected respondents using an
   ISO/IEC 25010 questionnaire covering functional suitability, usability,
   performance, reliability, and security.

Limitations

The project operates within clear, realistic boundaries:
1. No Electrical Grid Control: The system is an information and communication
   platform. It does not physically connect to substation circuit breakers,
   transformers, or automated smart meters, and it cannot turn electricity on
   or off automatically.
2. Internet Connectivity Requirement: Users need an active mobile data or Wi-Fi
   connection to submit new reports and receive live map updates. Although the
   PWA caches the app shell so it opens offline, reports filed without internet
   must wait until connection returns before they reach the server.
3. Geocoding Precision: Address lookups rely on OpenStreetMap Nominatim. In rural
   sitios or unpaved areas where street data is limited, users must confirm
   their purok manually.
4. SMS Delivery: Text message alerts depend on cellular signal coverage and
   available SMS credits on the Semaphore gateway. In local development or
   offline testing, the system logs simulated text messages instead.


Definition of Terms

The following terms are defined operationally based on how they are used in
this project:

Administrator - A user with full privileges to manage user accounts, review
system audit logs, configure global settings, and oversee database backups.

Barangay - The basic local government unit in Valencia City; used as the main
geographical boundary for grouping and reporting power outages.

Chatbot - An automated assistant in the resident portal that recognizes common
inquiries in English and Cebuano using keyword matching and offers quick answers
or page links.

Citizen Feedback - A feature where reporting residents submit a 1 to 5 star
rating and brief comments after service restoration to evaluate the repair.

Data Flow Diagram (DFD) - A structured diagram showing how information enters,
moves between processes, and gets stored in the Valencia PowerWatch system.

Estimated Time of Restoration (ETR) - An estimated date and time for power
restoration, calculated by considering fault category, report volume, and local
weather.

Entity Relationship Diagram (ERD) - A diagram representing database tables,
their primary and foreign keys, and the connections between them.

Field Personnel (Staff) - Linemen and technicians who receive repair tickets,
navigate to the reported location, and record job progress on the mobile staff
portal.

Geospatial Mapping - The visual presentation of outage locations and cluster
densities on an interactive map using geographic coordinates.

Incident (`OUT-XXXX`) - An official record created by dispatchers that represents
a verified power outage event, linking one or more individual resident reports.

ISO/IEC 25010 - An international standard used to evaluate software quality
across characteristics including usability, reliability, and functionality.

Outage Report (`VPR-XXXX`) - A ticket filed by a resident containing details of
a power loss, including barangay, purok, GPS coordinates, and optional photos.

Progressive Web Application (PWA) - A website built with modern web tools that
can be installed directly onto a mobile phone home screen and open quickly like
a native app.

Purok - A community zone within a barangay; used in this study to pinpoint the
neighborhood where an outage occurred.

Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) - A security rule ensuring users can only view
and edit pages and data permitted for their specific account type.

Semaphore API - A Philippine online SMS service used by the system backend to
send text message notifications to mobile numbers.

CHAPTER 2
REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE AND STUDIES
================================================================================

This chapter reviews relevant literature, published research, and existing
systems that support the design of Valencia PowerWatch. To provide a thorough
academic background, the review is organized into eight practical areas:
technology adoption models, civic crowdsourcing, outage management, geospatial
mapping, progressive web apps, SMS alerting, portal security, and restoration
time estimation. The chapter concludes with a synthesis identifying the local
research gap that this project addresses.

Related Literature

1. Theoretical Frameworks in Technology Adoption and System Success

When introducing new software to a community, understanding how people accept
and use technology is critical. DeLone and McLean (2003) updated their Information
Systems Success Model to show that system success depends on three key inputs:
System Quality (how well the software works), Information Quality (how clear and
accurate the data is), and Service Quality. These directly influence whether
people use the software and feel satisfied with it. In Valencia PowerWatch, System
Quality means fast loading times and a reliable interface on mobile phones.
Information Quality means providing accurate outage notices, verified incident
badges, and realistic restoration estimates. When residents see that reports
lead to actual updates, trust and satisfaction increase.

Similarly, Davis (1989) developed the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), proving
that user adoption is driven primarily by two beliefs: Perceived Usefulness (how
much the tool helps them in daily life) and Perceived Ease of Use (how simple it
is to learn and operate). Venkatesh and Davis (2000) later noted in TAM2 that
relevance to a user's immediate needs strongly reinforces usefulness. For a
family in Valencia experiencing an evening blackout, usefulness means being able
to check their phone and immediately learn if their feeder line tripped. Ease of
use means filing a report in three simple steps without filling out confusing
forms.

Carter and Belanger (2005) examined citizen adoption of digital public services
and found that trust is the decisive factor—both trust in the agency providing the
service and trust in the technical platform. If residents feel confident that the
reporting tool will not leak their personal phone numbers and that the utility
actually monitors incoming tickets, community adoption grows steadily.


2. Civic Reporting, Open311, and Citizen Participation

Civic reporting systems allow citizens to report local infrastructure issues
directly to municipal agencies. The Open311 consortium (Open311, n.d.) developed
a widely accepted open protocol (GeoReport v2) that standardizes how service
requests—such as broken streetlights and water leaks—are submitted and tracked.
The City of Bloomington's uReport (City of Bloomington, n.d.) is a practical
example of an open-source civic reporting tool built on these concepts. Valencia
PowerWatch follows similar principles by giving every ticket a unique tracking
code (`VPR-XXXX`), clear lifecycle stages, and geographic coordinates.

Offenhuber (2014) studied 311 systems in major cities and highlighted a common
failure point: reporting fatigue. When citizens submit complaints into an opaque
system that never provides feedback, they quickly stop using it. Valencia
PowerWatch prevents this by establishing clear feedback loops: as soon as a
dispatcher reviews a report or a lineman reaches the site, the system updates the
resident's timeline and sends an automated text alert.

In the Philippines, Fernandez et al. (2020) analyzed crowdsourced disaster
reporting in Metro Manila and noted that mobile citizen reporting significantly
improves response times during emergencies. However, they emphasized that systems
in provincial areas must be lightweight, work well over slower mobile networks,
and support local dialects so all community members can participate comfortably.


3. Outage Management Systems and Distribution Grid Operations

Electric utilities depend on Outage Management Systems (OMS) to identify grid
faults and dispatch repair crews. Brown (2008) in "Electric Power Distribution
Reliability" detailed how utilities evolved from paper trouble tickets to
computerized databases that link consumer calls with physical feeder maps. In
rural and provincial areas, however, complete smart meter installation (AMI) is
often too expensive, meaning utilities cannot automatically know when an
individual household loses power.

Farhangi (2010) explained that during the transition to smarter grids,
crowdsourced notifications from consumers serve as an affordable, human-driven
sensor network. When several households along the same road report power loss
at the same time, dispatchers can infer where a line tripped before a crew even
leaves the station. Kezunovic et al. (2012) added that combining trouble calls
with spatial mapping noticeably reduces outage duration metrics (such as SAIDI
and CAIDI). Valencia PowerWatch applies this by grouping neighborhood reports
into master incidents (`OUT-XXXX`), giving dispatchers an immediate snapshot of
which feeders have failed.


4. Geospatial Mapping and Location-Based Services in Public Utilities

Mapping plays a vital role in managing utility networks. Goodchild (2007)
introduced the concept of Volunteered Geographic Information (VGI), showing that
citizens on the ground can provide detailed, localized spatial information that
centralized offices often overlook. Esri's ArcGIS Survey123 (Esri, n.d.) is a
widely used example of a mobile data collection tool that links field forms to
digital maps.

While enterprise GIS platforms are powerful, their high licensing costs make them
impractical for smaller cooperatives and academic projects. Open-source tools
like Leaflet.js provide an excellent alternative, offering fast and flexible map
rendering inside web browsers. Haklay (2010) evaluated OpenStreetMap data and
found that community-contributed spatial data in populated areas is accurate and
frequently updated. Valencia PowerWatch uses Leaflet.js to render outage pins,
heatmaps, and official barangay boundaries, while OpenStreetMap Nominatim handles
reverse-geocoding to convert GPS coordinates into recognizable neighborhood names.


5. Progressive Web Applications in Low-Bandwidth Environments

Public platforms must be easy to access on all devices. Biørn-Hansen et al.
(2020) compared native mobile applications with Progressive Web Applications
(PWAs) and found that PWAs significantly lower user friction. Residents do not
need to open an app store, download large installation files, or worry about
device storage limits. A PWA runs directly inside the browser and can be added to
the phone's home screen with one tap.

PWAs use Service Workers to cache critical files locally on the device. Malavolta
et al. (2017) demonstrated that caching application shells allows web apps to
open almost instantly, even over slow 2G or 3G mobile connections commonly found
in provincial barangays. Valencia PowerWatch uses separate PWA configurations for
the Community Portal and the Staff Field App, ensuring quick access and offline
shell readiness for both residents and field technicians.


6. Multi-Channel Notifications and SMS Alerting in Emergency Response

Reliable communication is critical during power failures. While push
notifications and social media updates work well when internet access is strong,
Short Message Service (SMS) remains the most reliable alerting method in
provincial Philippine communities where mobile data can be spotty.

Al-Dmour et al. (2020) reviewed emergency alert systems and observed that text
messages achieve an open rate of over 90% within fifteen minutes of sending,
far exceeding emails or social media posts that get buried under platform
algorithms. In the Philippines, the Semaphore SMS Gateway API allows web
applications to send automated text messages directly to mobile numbers on all
major networks (Globe, Smart, DITO). Valencia PowerWatch uses Semaphore to send
immediate text alerts for key milestones, such as when a report is verified,
when a repair crew is on the way, and when power has been restored.


7. Role-Based Security and Privacy Protection in Municipal Portals

Systems that collect citizen identities, phone numbers, and location details
must protect this data responsibly. Sandhu et al. (1996) established the
standard model for Role-Based Access Control (RBAC), showing that assigning
permissions to specific roles (such as Resident, Staff, or Admin) prevents
unauthorized access and ensures users only see what they need to see.

The Open Web Application Security Project (OWASP, 2021) identifies Broken Access
Control and Cryptographic Failures as common web application risks. Under the
Philippine Data Privacy Act of 2012 (Republic Act No. 10173), personal contact
information must be stored securely. Valencia PowerWatch follows these best
practices by hashing user passwords with strong `scryptSync` algorithms, storing
session tokens as SHA-256 hashes, validating uploaded media files before saving
them, and enforcing server-side route guards on every private API endpoint.


8. Restoration Time Estimation and Heuristic Modeling

Giving residents a realistic Estimated Time of Restoration (ETR) reduces panic
and helps families plan during a blackout. Coster et al. (2011) showed that
outage duration depends primarily on three factors: the type of physical fault
(e.g., a simple blown fuse versus a fallen pole), weather severity (e.g., strong
winds or thunderstorms that slow down crew repairs), and the total number of
concurrent outages competing for repair teams.

While advanced machine learning models (Ji et al., 2016) have been studied,
they require years of historical maintenance data that local cooperatives rarely
have in digital format. In practice, a rule-based deterministic algorithm that
adjusts base repair times according to weather multipliers and report cluster
density offers a practical, transparent, and accurate solution. Valencia
PowerWatch uses a dedicated calculation module (`etr.js`) to produce dependable
estimates dynamically without heavy server computation.


Synthesis and Research Gap

The reviewed literature shows that modern outage management benefits greatly
from combining simple mobile interfaces (TAM, PWA), structured public reporting
(Open311), volunteer mapping (VGI, GIS), reliable text alerting (SMS), and
transparent restoration estimates (ETR).

Existing tools, however, leave a clear gap. International civic applications
like uReport handle general neighborhood issues but lack utility-specific
workflows like lineman dispatch and technical repair stages. Commercial utility
systems used by large private companies (such as Meralco in Metro Manila) are
costly enterprise platforms designed for urban smart grids, making them
unobtainable for provincial cooperatives. In Valencia City, residents and utility
workers still rely on busy phone hotlines and scattered Facebook comments.

This creates a clear local gap: Valencia City lacks an accessible, low-cost,
multi-role digital platform built specifically for its geographic and community
needs. Valencia PowerWatch fills this gap directly. It offers a bilingual,
easy-to-use reporting portal for residents, an incident-clustering dashboard for
dispatchers, and a mobile tracking tool for field crews, all supported by a
resilient database setup tailored for local operations.

CHAPTER 3
RESEARCH METHODOLOGY
================================================================================

This chapter explains the methods, research procedures, system architecture,
and technical specifications used to design, develop, and evaluate Valencia
PowerWatch. It details the research setting, research design, participants,
sampling technique, data gathering tools, statistical treatment, development
process, system flow, architecture, hardware and software requirements, Data
Flow Diagrams (DFD), and Entity Relationship Diagrams (ERD).

Research Setting

The study is conducted in Valencia City, a bustling component city in central
Bukidnon, Northern Mindanao (Region X), Philippines. Located at geographic
coordinates 7.906° N, 125.094° E, Valencia City spans an area of 587.29 square
kilometers, serving as the trade and agricultural center of the province.

Electrical power distribution across the city is operated by the First Bukidnon
Electric Cooperative, Inc. (FIBECO). Valencia City consists of thirty-one (31)
official barangays, ranging from busy urban areas like Poblacion, Lumbo,
Bagontaas, and Batangan, to large rural farming communities such as Guinoyuran,
Lilingayon, Mt. Nebo, and Concepcion.

These geographic differences mean that power faults in the city center can often
be reached quickly, while rural faults along unpaved or mountainous roads require
more coordination. This makes Valencia City an ideal setting for deploying and
testing a map-based reporting and field tracking system.

Research Design

This study uses a Developmental Research Design, based on the Design and
Development Research (DDR) model described by Richey and Klein (2007).
Developmental research focuses on the systematic planning, creation, testing,
and evaluation of a functional software solution that solves a concrete problem.

The study followed four sequential phases:
1. Requirements Analysis: Documenting current reporting pain points, user roles,
   and necessary system features through stakeholder consultations.
2. System Design: Developing user interface mockups, process flowcharts, DFDs,
   and database schemas.
3. Prototype Development: Writing and integrating the backend REST API, responsive
   web interfaces, mapping components, and SMS messaging services.
4. Testing and Evaluation: Conducting functional automated unit testing and
   measuring user acceptance using an ISO/IEC 25010 evaluation questionnaire.


Respondents and Sampling Technique

To obtain reliable and well-rounded feedback, the study involved fifty (50)
selected respondents representing four key stakeholder groups:

| Respondent Group               | Count | Description / Role in Evaluation                                    |
|--------------------------------|-------|---------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Community Residents            | 30    | Homeowners and local business owners across urban and rural zones   |
| Field Maintenance Linemen      | 10    | Utility technical workers who test mobile field dispatch features   |
| Utility Dispatchers / Admins   | 5     | Office staff who verify reports and manage overall incident tickets |
| IT Professionals / Engineers   | 5     | Software developers who evaluate code quality and architecture      |
| **Total Respondents**          | **50**|                                                                     |

The study used Purposive Sampling. This non-probability sampling method was
chosen because evaluation required participants who either live in Valencia City,
work directly in power maintenance, or possess technical knowledge in software
engineering to assess the system accurately against ISO/IEC 25010 standards.


Data Gathering and Research Instruments

Data was collected through qualitative and quantitative methods:
1. Semi-Structured Interviews: Conducted with residents, barangay leaders, and
   linemen to understand common reporting delays and verbal communication
   difficulties during blackouts.
2. Workflow Observation: Reviewing how telephone complaints are currently
   written down and passed to line crews.
3. Standardized Evaluation Questionnaire: After using the working prototype,
   respondents answered a survey based on the ISO/IEC 25010 Software Quality
   Standard. The survey evaluated five core criteria:
   - Functional Suitability: Whether the system provides the required reporting,
     verification, and dispatch capabilities.
   - Usability: How easy the system is to learn, navigate, and read on mobile
     and desktop screens.
   - Performance Efficiency: How quickly the app loads, displays map markers,
     and processes submissions.
   - Reliability: How consistently the system saves records, handles network
     interruptions, and preserves data.
   - Security: How effectively the system protects user passwords and restricts
     administrative pages to authorized personnel.

All respondents participated voluntarily with signed consent forms, and all
personal data was handled confidentially in accordance with Republic Act No. 10173
(Data Privacy Act of 2012).


Statistical Treatment of Data

Survey responses were tabulated and analyzed using basic descriptive statistics,
specifically the Weighted Mean (\(\bar{x}\)):

\[
\bar{x} = \frac{\sum (f \cdot x)}{N}
\]

Where:
- \(\bar{x}\) = Weighted Mean
- \(f\) = Frequency of responses for each rating
- \(x\) = Scale value (1 to 5)
- \(N\) = Total number of respondents (50)

To interpret the results, the following 5-point Likert Scale continuum was
used:

| Scale | Mean Range  | Qualitative Description  | Verbal Interpretation                 |
|:-----:|:-----------:|:-------------------------|:--------------------------------------|
| 5     | 4.21 – 5.00 | Strongly Agree           | Highly Acceptable / Excellent         |
| 4     | 3.41 – 4.20 | Agree                    | Acceptable / Very Satisfactory        |
| 3     | 2.61 – 3.40 | Neutral                  | Moderately Acceptable / Satisfactory  |
| 2     | 1.81 – 2.60 | Disagree                 | Poor / Needs Improvement              |
| 1     | 1.00 – 1.80 | Strongly Disagree        | Very Poor / Unacceptable              |


System Development Methodology

The project followed the Agile Scrum Development Methodology. Agile was
chosen because civic web applications benefit from frequent iterations, quick
testing, and continuous feedback from target users.

Work was organized into four 2-week sprints:
- Sprint 1 (Core Foundation): Database design, user registration, scrypt password
  security, and base administrative views.
- Sprint 2 (Citizen Reporting & Mapping): Resident portal, GPS coordinate
  capture, photo uploads with validation, and barangay reverse-geocoding.
- Sprint 3 (Field Dispatch & Alerts): Mobile staff portal, job stage tracking
  (En Route, Inspecting, Repairing), on-site photo evidence, and Semaphore SMS.
- Sprint 4 (Incident Linking, Chatbot & Polish): Grouping reports into master
  incidents, deterministic ETR calculation, Cebuano chatbot, automated unit tests
  (96 tests passing), and cloud deployment on Render.com.

System Flow

The operational flow of Valencia PowerWatch follows a straightforward sequence
from report submission to resolution:

1. Submitting a Report: A resident opens the Community Portal on their mobile
   phone or computer, selects their barangay, indicates their purok, marks their
   location on the map, chooses the suspected fault type, attaches a photo if
   available, and submits ticket `VPR-XXXX`.
2. Intake and Verification: The server validates the upload, logs the submission
   time, and places the ticket in the dispatcher's review queue.
3. Grouping into Incidents: The dispatcher reviews incoming tickets on the Admin
   Dashboard. If several reports come from the same neighborhood or line feeder,
   the dispatcher links them under a single master Incident (`OUT-XXXX`). The
   system's ETR algorithm automatically calculates an estimated repair time based
   on current weather and fault type.
4. Field Crew Dispatch: The dispatcher assigns a field repair team to the
   incident. The system automatically sends an SMS notification to the lead
   lineman.
5. Field Repair Tracking: The linemen open the Staff Portal on their phones, check
   the location and attached photos, confirm arrival via GPS, and advance the
   job status (En Route -> Inspecting -> Repairing).
6. Completion and Citizen Feedback: Once repairs are done, the crew uploads an
   after-repair photo and marks the job complete. The dispatcher marks the master
   incident as "Resolved," which automatically triggers text messages and in-app
   alerts to all reporting residents. Residents can then open their portal to
   submit a 1 to 5 star rating confirming that their electricity is back on.

System Architecture

Valencia PowerWatch is built on a 3-tier client-server architecture with a
hybrid data storage setup:

1. Presentation Tier (Client Portals):
   - Community Portal (`community.html`): Mobile-friendly interface for residents
     featuring a report wizard, Leaflet outage map, weather widget, and bilingual
     chatbot.
   - Admin SCADA Dashboard (`admin.html`): Full desktop dashboard for dispatchers
     with real-time alert sounds, incident grouping queues, user management, and
     analytics.
   - Staff Field Portal (`staff.html`): Lightweight, standalone mobile PWA for
     linemen with offline shell caching and fast touch controls.
2. Application Tier (Node.js & Express Server):
   - Hosted in production on Render.com.
   - Manages secure user authentication using `scryptSync` password hashing and
     SHA-256 session token hashes.
   - Enforces Role-Based Access Control (`requireRole`) across all private API
     routes.
   - Runs the deterministic ETR calculation module (`etr.js`).
   - Handles address matching against the 31 official Valencia barangays
     (`location-geocoder.js`).
   - Drives the rule-based Cebuano and English chatbot (`chatbot.js`).
3. Data and Persistence Tier (Hybrid Dual-Store):
   - Local Transactional Database: High-speed local SQLite database running in
     Write-Ahead Logging (WAL) mode for fast reads and writes.
   - Cloud Durability Engine: An automated background process (`persistence.js`)
     that regularly compresses SQLite snapshots and uploads them to a Neon
     Serverless PostgreSQL database. This ensures no data is lost when cloud
     server containers restart.
4. External Service Integrations:
   - Semaphore SMS API: Sends automated text alerts to Philippine mobile numbers.
   - OpenStreetMap & Leaflet: Delivers map tiles and geographic markers.
   - Open-Meteo Weather API: Supplies live weather data for ETR calculations.

Data Flow Diagram (DFD)

Context Diagram (Level 0)

The Context Diagram shows Valencia PowerWatch as a single system interacting
with four external entities:
- Residents: Submit outage reports, feedback ratings, and chatbot questions;
  receive ticket tracking updates, SMS notifications, and public advisories.
- Field Linemen: Receive repair assignments and location coordinates; send
  job stage updates, GPS arrival confirmations, and completion photos.
- Dispatchers and Administrators: Receive incoming report queues, audit logs,
  and outage summaries; send incident groups, repair assignments, and official
  announcements.
- External Services: The Semaphore SMS API and OpenStreetMap receive notification
  requests and geocoding queries, returning delivery statuses and location names.

Level 1 Data Flow Diagram

The Level 1 DFD breaks the system down into six main processes:
- Process 1.0 (Authentication & Roles): Validates login credentials against the
  `users` and `sessions` stores.
- Process 2.0 (Report Intake & Geocoding): Accepts report details, checks media
  files, and records tickets in `outage_reports` and `report_status_history`.
- Process 3.0 (Incident Verification): Allows dispatchers to review reports and
  combine them into master `outage_incidents`.
- Process 4.0 (Dispatch Management): Manages crew assignments through `repair_teams`
  and logs stage progress in `repair_assignment_updates`.
- Process 5.0 (ETR & Map Processing): Calculates restoration estimates via
  `etr.js` and maps data against the 31 official barangay boundaries.
- Process 6.0 (Alerts & Citizen Feedback): Dispatches text alerts via `sms_logs`
  and saves post-restoration ratings in `citizen_feedback`.

Entity Relationship Diagram (ERD)

The system uses 26 relational tables in SQLite, which are mapped to an expanded
35-table schema in Neon PostgreSQL for long-term scalability. The main entities
and their relationships are:

1. `users`: Stores user accounts, encrypted passwords (`password_hash`), contact
   numbers, and user roles (`resident`, `personnel`, `utility`, `administrator`).
   - One user can have multiple active `sessions`.
   - One user can file multiple `outage_reports` as `reporter_id`.
   - One user can perform multiple actions recorded in `audit_logs`.
2. `barangays`: Contains the 31 official barangays of Valencia City with their
   center geographic coordinates (latitude and longitude).
   - Referenced by reports, incidents, and staff territory permissions.
3. `outage_reports`: Stores individual citizen complaints with unique codes
   (`VPR-XXXX`), GPS coordinates, barangay, purok, reported fault type, and
   verification status.
   - Has a one-to-many relationship with `report_attachments` (uploaded photos).
   - Has a one-to-many relationship with `report_status_history` (audit trail).
   - Links to `outage_incidents` through `incident_id` and the `incident_links`
     table.
4. `outage_incidents`: Represents master outage events created by dispatchers
   with codes (`OUT-XXXX`), priority levels, customer counts, and computed ETR.
   - Links to affected barangays through the `incident_areas` junction table.
   - Links to field repair tasks through `repair_assignments`.
5. `repair_teams`: Records maintenance crews, crew leaders, contact numbers, and
   vehicle details.
   - Linked to `repair_assignments` via `team_id`.
6. `repair_assignments`: Tracks dispatched jobs, assigned teams, target puroks,
   and completion timestamps.
   - Has a one-to-many relationship with `repair_assignment_evidence`.
   - Has a one-to-many relationship with `repair_assignment_updates`.
7. `citizen_feedback`: Records post-repair satisfaction ratings (1 to 5 stars)
   tied to specific reports and users.
8. `sms_logs`: Maintains an audit trail of all text messages sent through the
   Semaphore API.
